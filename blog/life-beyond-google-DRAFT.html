The short version:

  • The first three layers of the Find/Understand/Trust framework taught machines to read your business. The fourth layer asks whether they can act on it.
  • This is not a new family of decisions. It is the next loop of one you have made before — like the year you decided whether to put your catalog online, whether to publish prices, and whether to actually transact through the website.
  • The new vocabulary (APIs, MCPs, A2A, /.well-known/) maps onto things you already know. EDI. The press kit. Broker-to-broker negotiation. The front-desk directory. The shape is familiar; the audience is new.
  • The hard part is who decides what your business will let agents do. If you do not make the call, your CDN, your AI platform vendor, and your eCommerce stack will make it for you. Several of them already are.

The Next Loop of a Decision You've Made Before

Think back to roughly 2010.

If you ran a B2B catalog business, an industrial distributor, a professional services firm, or any operation that sold to other businesses, you were making three decisions at once. Whether to put your catalog online at all. Whether to show pricing publicly. And whether to actually sell through the website, or just use it as a brochure and route the order through a salesperson.

None of those decisions felt small. Pricing visibility is a competitive question. Selling online means restructuring how you handle terms, returns, and credit. Putting your catalog where Google can index it means a stranger can find it without ever meeting your sales team. Plenty of businesses delayed those decisions for years. Many made some but not others. A few never made them at all and watched marketplaces and search engines make the calls for them — sometimes painfully.

The web changed the audience your business had to publish for. It changed who could find you, on what schedule, with what information in hand. The decisions were not new in shape. Salesmen and Yellow Pages and trade-show booklets had asked the same questions in different forms for decades. The web was just the latest loop.

The agent-mediated web is the next loop.

This time the audience is not a buyer with a browser. It is an AI agent acting on behalf of a buyer. Sometimes representing a procurement officer running a sourcing list. Sometimes representing a homeowner researching contractors. Sometimes representing the buyer's own ChatGPT or Gemini that has been asked to "find three quotes and bring me the cheapest one with a decent reputation." The agent reads, weighs, recommends, and — increasingly — acts. Books the demo. Pulls the quote. Places the order.

This article is about that fourth layer. We call it USE in the Find/Understand/Trust framework: can an AI agent do anything with you once it is there? It is the same family of decisions you made about the public web in 2010. It just expands the audience by one more participant.

The good news is you have done this kind of work before. The harder news is that the platform vendors are going to compress your timeline, and they are going to start making the calls for the businesses that do not decide.

From One Surface to Many

The first three layers of this framework have a clear logic. FIND asks: can a machine access your content? UNDERSTAND asks: does the machine know what your business does? TRUST asks: does it have enough corroboration to mention you without hedging?

All three describe a machine that is reading you. Reading and citing. Reading and summarizing. Reading and recommending. The work on your end is making sure your site is legible and trustworthy enough that the machine does not get the story wrong.

USE describes a different relationship. The machine is no longer reading. It is acting.

That is the simplest way to say what has changed. Up until very recently, the most an AI platform did with your website was describe it back to a human. Now AI agents are taking actions: scheduling appointments, pulling quotes, placing orders, opening tickets, submitting forms, completing transactions, moving money. The web is shifting from a place machines read to a place machines act.

Before we go further, a word about surfaces. The word means something specific in agent-readiness, and the rest of this article rests on it. A surface is a defined access point on your website built for a specific type of visitor, with a contract about what that visitor can do there. Your customer-facing web pages are a surface — built for human readers, with HTML and CSS designed for eyes and screen readers. An API endpoint is a different surface — built for software, with structured data designed to be parsed by code. An MCP server is a third surface — built for AI agents, with a press-kit-style description of what the business is and what an agent can do on its behalf. A /.well-known/ entry is a fourth — built for discovery, pointing other agents to the other surfaces.

A physical retailer already has multiple surfaces today. The customer-facing storefront is one. The receiving dock at the back is a different surface — built for delivery trucks, with a loading-bay shape, a check-in process, and a different kind of receptionist. The wholesale-buyer entrance is a third. The vendor portal where suppliers submit invoices is a fourth. Same business, four surfaces, each shaped for who it serves and what they are allowed to do there. The agent-mediated web is doing the same thing to your website. The customer-facing pages stay. New surfaces get added next to them.

Where do they physically live? Some surfaces live inside your normal pages (schema markup embedded in the HTML). Some live at the root of your domain (/.well-known/). Some live at sister addresses (api.yoursite.com, or mcp.yoursite.com). The /.well-known/ directory is the index that tells an agent which surfaces your business has and where to find each one.

An agent doing those things needs a different surface than a reader does. Reading is forgiving: an AI summarizing your services page can guess, hedge, or skip what it cannot parse. Acting is unforgiving. To book a demo on a buyer's behalf, an agent needs a documented way to do that — an endpoint, an authentication handshake, a confirmation contract, a defined boundary of what it is allowed to do.

That is the work the fourth layer covers. In the Roadmap framework, the four-C alliteration stays intact: Clear, Complete, Consistent, Capable. CAPABLE is the directive on your end. USE is the verb on the buyer's end. Same layer, two angles.

One number to anchor why this matters now. Cloudflare Radar's April 2026 reading puts bot traffic at 32% of all HTTP requests, and AI crawlers at 22% of all bot traffic — the second-largest category behind search. Today, 89.4% of that AI crawler traffic is training or mixed-purpose; only 2.2% is serving a live user query. The volume is in. The action layer is what flips that ratio from "machines learning about you" to "machines acting on you."

That 2.2% number is small enough to be worth slowing down for. Today, when a buyer asks ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, or Perplexity for "three industrial distributors near Indianapolis who carry stainless fittings," the platform is not answering from training data. Training data is what the model learned when it was originally built — months to a year out of date, with no specific knowledge of what is happening in your business today. So the platform conducts a live web search: ChatGPT through its browse tool, Claude through its native search capability, Gemini through Google grounding, Perplexity as a default mode. Most of the 89.4% training-or-mixed-purpose number is that live-search traffic — crawlers building the index that gets queried at runtime, plus crawlers fetching individual pages on demand when a question needs a fresh page. The platforms are reading.

The 2.2% is the slice that is not reading. It is an agent fetching a specific endpoint to do something — pull a current price, place an order, look up inventory in real time, complete a transaction. That is the action layer. It is small now because the action surfaces do not exist on most sites yet. The 2.2% is the leading edge of a ratio that will tip — measured in months, not years — as more sites publish agent surfaces. The work of this article is being ready for the tip.

The Four New Words You Need to Know

The fourth layer comes with four pieces of vocabulary that probably are not on your team's whiteboard yet: API, MCP, A2A, and /.well-known/. They sound new. The shapes they describe are not.

API as the modern EDI

In the 1980s and '90s, the biggest industrial distributors had EDI — Electronic Data Interchange — set up with their largest customers. A structured way for one company's ordering system to talk to another company's ordering system without a human typing anything in. Most businesses never touched it. It was set up between IT departments for the top-ten accounts. The smaller customers still phoned in or faxed orders.

An API — Application Programming Interface — is the modern, general-purpose version of EDI. It is how one piece of software talks to another, automatically, with no human in the middle. When a software vendor says "we have an API," that is what they mean. The vendor publishes a contract: here is how to ask us for a product list, a price, an order status, an inventory level. Any other piece of software that follows the contract can ask. The difference from EDI is mostly scope — EDI was bilateral and bespoke; modern APIs are general-purpose and documented for anyone authorized to use them.

An AI agent that wants to do something with your business is going to look for an API. If you have one published and documented, the agent can act. If you do not, the agent treats your site like a phone-and-fax customer — slow, manual, easily routed around.

MCP as the press kit

When journalists wrote about companies, the company gave the journalist a press kit. Vetted, structured, current information — what the company does, who runs it, what they offer, what to attribute to whom — so the journalist could represent the company accurately without interviewing the founder every time. Smart marketing teams kept the press kit current. Lazy ones let it go stale and watched the resulting articles get half the facts wrong.

MCP — Model Context Protocol — is the press kit for AI agents. Released by Anthropic in November 2024 and adopted across the industry through 2025, MCP is a standardized format for telling an agent, in machine-readable form, what your business does, what it sells, how to act on its behalf, and what it will allow. An "MCP server" is the press-kit server: a small, scoped service published at a known address that an agent can query when it needs to understand or act on your business. Anthropic's own framing was that MCP is "the USB-C of AI integrations" — a single standard plug that replaces the bespoke wiring every integration used to require.

Underneath the press-kit framing, MCP is technically an API. It speaks JSON over standard transport protocols — same plumbing as any modern API. What makes MCP different from a regular API is what gets bundled into the standard. A regular API just gives an agent endpoints, inputs, outputs, and an authentication scheme; every API publisher writes their own documentation in their own format and an agent confronted with three different APIs has to learn three different voices before it can use any of them. MCP standardizes the surrounding scaffolding. When an agent connects to an MCP server, the server self-describes in a structured way every MCP-aware agent can already parse: here are my resources (read-only data I can hand you — your catalog, your case studies, your inventory levels), here are my tools (actions you can take through me — place an order, submit an RFP, schedule a meeting), here are my prompts (templates I support — the workflow for booking a demo through us), and here are the permissions on each. The agent does not read documentation. It asks. The plumbing is the same as any API. The standardization is what changed.

A given MCP server can expose any combination of those three. A "data MCP" just publishes resources for the agent to draw on. An "action MCP" just publishes tools. Most production MCP servers expose both. The decision about which combination to publish is the same business decision as the one about your customer-facing pages — what do you let visitors see, what do you let them do, where do you draw the line.

One variant worth knowing about: WebMCP. WebMCP is a newer proposal that brings MCP capabilities into the browser context. Instead of running a separate MCP server somewhere on the network, a WebMCP-enabled website declares its MCP-style affordances directly in the page itself, so an agent running inside a user's browser (a Chrome AI Mode session, a Copilot extension, an in-browser assistant) can use those capabilities through the browser's own infrastructure. Lower friction for the site owner because there is no separate service to host. Higher friction for agents that are not browser-based. Both variants advertise their existence in /.well-known/, which is why the audit treats them the same way — the only thing that matters is that the agent can find an MCP-style surface when it goes looking.

Forrester predicts 30% of enterprise app vendors will launch their own MCP servers in 2026. If they are right, MCP is on track to become the press-kit-for-agents the way schema.org became the press-kit-for-search.

A2A as broker-to-broker negotiation

When a buyer's procurement officer talks to a seller's salesperson, that is one human representing each side, negotiating terms within the authority each one has been granted. When two software agents do the same thing — one representing the buyer, one representing the seller — that is A2A. Agent-to-agent. Google announced the A2A protocol in April 2025 specifically for this case, with more than 50 launch partners.

The shape is familiar. Travel agents routing requests between airlines, hotels, and customers. Brokers placing trades on behalf of clients. Customs agents negotiating with shipping agents. Two specialists representing two principals, working out a deal. A2A is the protocol version of that pattern, designed for software.

The payment-protocol layer is the letter-of-credit and escrow layer of the same deal. When neither side directly trusts the other to execute payment, you bring in a third party. Stripe and Visa and Mastercard are publishing payment protocols designed for agent-mediated transactions, including ACP — the Agentic Commerce Protocol, co-developed by OpenAI and Stripe and launched in 2025 — so an agent on the buyer side can negotiate a deal with an agent on the seller side and have the payment execute trustably even when neither has seen the other before.

/.well-known/ as the front-desk directory

Before the web, businesses listed themselves in Yellow Pages, Thomas Register, the trade association directory, the chamber of commerce roster. A known place where someone looking for them would check first. The /.well-known/ folder is the same idea, ported to the agent-mediated web.

It is a directory at the root of your domain — yoursite.com/.well-known/ — defined by IETF standards RFC 5785 and RFC 8615, where machines look first to find what your business publishes. Where is your API documentation? Your MCP server card? Your authentication endpoint? Your agent-skills index? Your payment protocol descriptor? All of it gets advertised in /.well-known/.

If you have never opened it, you are not alone. /.well-known/ has historically been a folder for engineering — SSL renewal, security disclosure, OAuth discovery — not marketing. That is changing. As of 2026, almost every protocol shipped to support agent-action surfaces publishes its address at /.well-known/. The folder has quietly become the agent's front-desk directory. The ownership inside the company is moving with it.

A quick note on llms.txt, since the question comes up. llms.txt was a 2024 proposal by Jeremy Howard (fast.ai): a single markdown file at the root of your domain giving LLMs a curated summary of your site, optimized for LLM consumption. It never got formal protocol adoption. None of the major AI platforms committed to using it for citation or ranking. Google said explicitly on May 15, 2026 that llms.txt is one of the practices site owners can safely ignore for generative-AI search optimization. SE Ranking's early-2026 analysis found roughly 10% of the domains in their sample had published one. If /.well-known/ is set up properly — with your MCP server card, api-catalog entry, agent-skills index, and OAuth discovery — you do not also need llms.txt. The agents and platforms that matter are going to /.well-known/.

One piece of framing worth ending this section on. Chatbots got humans used to dealing with machines in a more interactive way. There is no question that /.well-known/ is not going to give you a ranking or citation boost. But without it, whether or not an AI agent stumbles over your MCP or your A2A capability is a guess. And now that you understand how literal machines are from your personal dealings with chatbots, you have an idea of how literal you must be in training your website's agent surface on what visiting agents can and cannot do on your behalf. The chatbot taught you what literal feels like from the user side. /.well-known/ asks you to be that literal from the operator side.

What to Look At on Your Own Site

If you ran a 2010 catalog audit on your own site, you would ask roughly four questions. Is the catalog online? Are the prices visible? Can a customer transact directly? Does the site work without a salesperson in the loop?

The 2026 agent-action audit asks a parallel set. Here is what to look at, framed in business terms first.

Do you have an API? If a buyer's AI agent wants to pull a price, a quote, an inventory level, or a delivery date, is there a documented way to ask? Most B2B websites do not have one. The ones that do often have it buried in developer documentation, set up for partner integrations, and not advertised in any place an agent would think to look. The audit equivalent at the touchpoint level is the API Catalog check — an entry at /.well-known/api-catalog that publishes the APIs you have and how to reach them.

Have you published a press kit for agents? If a buyer's agent wants to know what your business does, what you sell, what claims you will stand behind, and how to act on your behalf — is there a structured, current source it can read? That is the MCP Server Card check (at /.well-known/mcp.json), backed by the Agent Skills Index check (at /.well-known/agent-skills/index.json) that lists specific things an agent is authorized to do on your behalf.

Can an agent authenticate on a customer's behalf? This is the modern equivalent of the customer self-service login. The audit equivalents are the OAuth/OIDC Discovery and OAuth Protected Resource checks — standardized ways for an agent to authenticate before it acts.

Have you decided whether you trust the agent before you let it act? This is the customer-credit-check question, ported to the agent era. The audit equivalent is the Web Bot Authentication check — cryptographic signing keys published at /.well-known/http-message-signatures-directory that let your systems verify which agent is asking before they answer.

Have you mapped the customer journey through to action, not just through to information? The audit equivalent is the WebMCP check — whether the agent can complete a transaction end-to-end on your site, or whether it has to hand off to a human at some step that was not documented.

Six touchpoints. Each one is a /.well-known/ entry. Each one was zero work for marketing six months ago and is becoming non-optional now. None of them is on your site if you have not put it there.

The Business Decisions Behind Those Touchpoints

Each of those six touchpoints is a technical artifact. What it carries is a business decision. The shape of the decision is the same shape you faced in 2010 about the catalog and the prices.

What do you let agents see? The 2010 version was "do we publish our pricing online." The 2026 version is "what surface do we expose at the API." Inventory but not customer-tier pricing? Public catalog but not contract-account pricing? Real-time availability, or yesterday's stock? Every API surface is a publishing decision. Most B2B sellers do not realize they are making one until a competitor's agent surface is already returning data theirs does not.

What do you let agents do? The 2010 version was "do we transact online, or just publish the catalog." The 2026 version is the same question with more granularity. Can an agent place an order? Pull a quote? Open a ticket? Negotiate terms? The MCP Server Card and the Agent Skills Index are where you draw that line — what the agent can do, and what the agent has to hand back to a human.

Which agents do you trust to do it? A 2010 catalog did not ask who the visitor was. Anyone with a browser could see the page. Agent-mediated sites are starting to ask. Web Bot Authentication is the mechanism. The decision is yours: do you let any agent transact, or only agents you have explicitly enrolled? Both answers are defensible. Not deciding is a decision your CDN will make for you.

How do you stand behind a price an agent quoted? The 2010 version of this question was whether to publish prices at all. The 2026 version is harder: prices on an agent surface are quotable, transactable, and held to the second they are issued. Inventory caching that was fine for a web visitor is not fine for a buyer's agent. Your business has to decide what the contract is — and your eCommerce platform has to be configured to honor it.

These are decisions only your business can make. They are not engineering choices. They are commercial, legal, and brand decisions that the technical work then executes.

The Decisions Platform Vendors Will Make For You

Here is the part of the 2010 loop most businesses did not see coming. The companies that delayed their decisions did not get to make them later. The decisions got made for them, by platform vendors that needed to publish answers regardless.

Amazon decided pricing visibility for thousands of brands by listing the product anyway. Google decided what your business was called by parsing your site and your reviews. Yelp decided what a customer's first impression of your restaurant looked like. None of those vendors asked permission. They published, and the businesses that had not decided in time inherited whatever the platform vendor chose.

The same thing is starting at the agent layer. Three places to watch.

Your CDN. Cloudflare, Akamai, AWS CloudFront, Fastly, and the smaller players have all shipped bot-management consoles that ask the website owner to decide which AI crawlers and which agents to allow, on what terms. The default settings are vendor-chosen. If your marketing team does not make the call, your DevOps team will pick whichever default keeps the security team happy — and that default is increasingly "block agents we do not recognize." Cloudflare's own data shows AI crawler traffic at 22% of all bot traffic, and default policies vary widely from one customer account to another. The decision is being made daily. The marketer's name is rarely on it.

Your AI platform vendor. When a buyer's ChatGPT or Gemini or Claude is deciding what to recommend or how to act on a buyer's behalf, the platform vendor is making the editorial call. OpenAI's Apps SDK launch list named seven first-wave partners. The companies not on that list are competing for inclusion later, on terms the platform vendor sets. If your industry's first wave is already named, your runway is shorter than the industry-priority table makes it look.

Your eCommerce or commerce platform. Shopify, BigCommerce, and the major B2B commerce platforms are publishing agent-readiness defaults — what they expose to agents, what they hold back, how prices flow, what inventory states they advertise. If you are on one of those platforms, you have already inherited a posture you did not choose. The work is finding out what your posture is and deciding whether it is the one you want.

The agent economy itself is not slowing down. Gartner predicts 15% of day-to-day work decisions will be made autonomously through agentic AI by 2028, up from effectively 0% in 2024, and 33% of enterprise software applications will include agentic AI by 2028, up from less than 1% in 2024. The hype around the loud first wave of internal agent builds is over-bought. The shift underneath it is not. The businesses that come out of this loop with a working agent surface are not going to be the ones with the biggest tech budget. They are going to be the ones who decided early.

From Use Back to the Foundation

I called this Life Beyond Google for a reason. Not Life After Google. Google is not going anywhere. It is still going to be one of the places a buyer asks a question. Beyond means there are more places now — ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Perplexity, vendor-specific agents, MCP servers in the buyer's own stack — and the decision about how your business gets represented and acted on has to be made across all of them.

The Find/Understand/Trust/Use framework is the simplest map I have for that decision.

FIND asks: can the agent access your content? CDN posture, robots.txt, JavaScript rendering, structured data. Binary. You pass or you do not.

UNDERSTAND asks: does the agent comprehend what your business does? Entity clarity, fact density, semantic structure, schema coverage. The clarity layer.

TRUST asks: does the agent have enough corroboration to cite you with confidence? Cross-source consistency, freshness, third-party verification. The reputation layer.

USE asks: can the agent do anything with you once it is there? Documented APIs, MCP server cards, OAuth discovery, Web Bot Authentication, payment protocols. The action layer.

USE rests on the first three. Foundation first. If an agent cannot find you, cannot understand you, or does not trust you, the most beautifully published /.well-known/ folder in the world does not matter. The agent never gets to it. Build the foundation, then publish the action surface. In that order.

The amount of work behind that sentence is the magnitude-of-work conversation. The 2010 catalog-online decision was not a weekend. It took distributors years, and the platform vendors took a cut from everyone who waited. This loop is going to compress that timeline. The family of decisions is the same. The deadline is shorter.

The companies that come out the other side with a working agent surface — and a foundation strong enough to support it — are the ones cited when the industry-priority table for their row finally gets numbers behind it. Right now, in the slower rows, those positions are still wide open.

Decide early. The platform vendors are publishing defaults daily, and the defaults are not going to land in your favor.

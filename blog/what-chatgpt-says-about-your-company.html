What Does ChatGPT Say About Your Company? (And Is It True?)

Open ChatGPT right now. Type your company name in a new chat. Let it respond.

Read the answer carefully. How much of it is actually true?

If you're like most B2B company leaders we've tested, you're going to feel something between disappointment and vertigo. ChatGPT will describe your company with complete confidence. It will sound plausible. It might even sound impressive. And almost none of it will be verifiable.

This is the core problem nobody talks about. AI systems aren't refusing to answer. They're not saying "I don't have data on your company." Instead, they're filling the silence with pattern-matched language that sounds like facts but isn't grounded in anything specific.

We call these "vibes."

The Fact vs. Vibe Framework

We've profiled 120 B2B companies across four major AI platforms (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Perplexity) and scored their responses using a simple framework.

Facts are verifiable claims. They name specific services. They mention real leaders or locations. They reference measurable results. They're things the AI could only know from sources that actually document your business.

Examples of facts:

  • "Their founding team includes [real person's name], formerly [verifiable role]"
  • "They specialize in [specific, niche service you actually offer]"
  • "Their largest client is [named company] in [measurable vertical]"
  • "They've published research showing [specific methodology or results]"

Vibes are pattern-matched filler. They sound plausible. They use industry language. They're confident. But they describe nearly everyone in your space.

Examples of vibes:

  • "They provide innovative solutions to complex business challenges"
  • "They help companies optimize their digital transformation"
  • "They're committed to delivering excellence and driving results"
  • "They serve mid-market and enterprise clients across multiple industries"

Vibes aren't wrong. They're just not specific. A competitor three states away could be described in almost identical language and the AI would never know the difference.

The scoring is simple: (facts × 2) - (vibes × 2).

If you score positive, the AI system actually knows something about you. If you score negative, it's making it up.

Why This Happens (It's Not Malicious)

AI systems aren't hallucinating out of malice. They're doing exactly what they were built to do: pattern matching.

During training, these models absorbed vast amounts of text from the web. They learned that certain phrases correlate with certain industries. They understand that B2B service companies often describe themselves with language around "solutions," "optimization," "transformation," and "results." They've internalized the patterns of marketing speak.

When you ask ChatGPT about your company, here's what happens: The system looks for patterns it recognizes. It finds nothing specific (your company probably isn't mentioned prominently on thousands of indexed pages). So it fills the gap by matching patterns.

It's doing inference, not retrieval. It's not lying. It's pattern-matching. The problem is that pattern-matching is indistinguishable from lying when it sounds confident.

The AI system has no way to distinguish between "I don't know anything about this company" and "I've seen a thousand descriptions like this and I'll synthesize one." Both feel like answers to the AI. Both feel like knowledge to the reader.

The training data for these models is also static. Most were trained on information that's 1 to 3 years old. If you've made significant changes, rebranded, shifted your service offerings, or grown your team, the AI probably doesn't know. It's working with a blurry, outdated image of your business. And it fills in the missing details with vibes.

The Platform Differences Matter

Not all AI platforms handle unknown information the same way.

ChatGPT defaults to confidence. When it doesn't have facts, it still generates detailed-sounding answers. The tone is conversational and assured. Users tend to trust it because it never hedges. This is actually the most dangerous posture because it creates the illusion of knowledge.

Claude tends to be more cautious in phrasing. It uses more conditional language. But we found cases where this caution masked identity confusion. Claude would confidently describe the wrong entity using the exact same careful-sounding structure. The hedge didn't prevent hallucination. It just made hallucination sound more trustworthy.

Gemini produces longer, more verbose responses. More words created more opportunities to include facts, but instead it just meant more vibes. The extra length made the lack of specificity harder to spot.

Perplexity actually solves this problem by citing sources. When you ask Perplexity about your company, you get back links to the pages it pulled facts from. You can verify instantly. This is why Perplexity's scores are so much better. It's not relying on pattern matching when live search is available.

For most companies, though, the experience is the same across all platforms: confident-sounding descriptions that don't actually describe the business.

What This Means for Your Business

This matters because buyers are about to start asking AI systems for recommendations.

Imagine this scenario: A prospect is evaluating three companies in your space. They don't know any of you. So they ask Claude: "Tell me about leading agencies in [your vertical]."

Claude returns descriptions of all three. One description is fact-dense. It names specific clients, references published case studies, mentions a recognized founder. The prospect checks those facts. They're all verifiable.

The other two descriptions sound equally professional. They use the same confident tone. But when the prospect tries to verify them, they can't. There are no named clients. No published results. No recognizable people. No external evidence. Just vibes.

Which company do you think gets the follow-up meeting?

This is already happening. It will accelerate. AI-powered recommendation engines will become standard. Your ability to be described in verifiable, fact-dense language will become a competitive asset.

Right now, most B2B companies are invisible to AI systems. We're in a window where this still feels abstract. But the infrastructure is being built. The platforms are getting smarter about integrating search. Enterprise AI tools are beginning to require citation chains. Companies that show up as facts in AI responses will have a structural advantage.

Companies that only exist as vibes will be functionally invisible.

How to Know Your Score

The easiest way to find out is to test. Open ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, or Perplexity. Ask about your company. Read the response carefully.

Score it using the framework:

  • Look for verifiable claims (facts). These are specific enough that you could Google them and confirm they're true.
  • Look for generic, could-describe-anyone language (vibes). These are confident but interchangeable with other companies' descriptions.

Count each. Multiply facts by 2. Multiply vibes by 2. Subtract vibes from facts.

  • Positive score: The AI knows you exist in some form.
  • Negative score: The AI is pattern-matching. It's describing your category, not your company.

Why Vibes Happen (The Deeper Layer)

There's a technical reason this happens, and it connects to something we'll explore more deeply in future posts.

AI systems don't know what they don't know. They can't distinguish between "I was trained on data about this company" and "This phrase pattern matches my training data, so I'll generate something that fits the pattern."

Both feel like knowledge to the model. Both produce text that looks like output. The difference is that one is actually retrieval and one is synthesis. To the AI, there's no meaningful distinction.

What the AI really lacks is structure.

When you ask ChatGPT about your company, it's searching through vectors of unstructured text. Your company might exist in that training data as scattered mentions across blog posts, LinkedIn profiles, news articles, and your own website. But that data is fragmented. The AI has to synthesize across fragments and fill gaps.

The better structured your business presence is, the fewer gaps the AI has to fill. But we'll get into that more deeply in the next pillar post.

For now, the core insight is simple: If your business doesn't appear as specific, verifiable facts in response to AI queries, you're competing at a disadvantage. Your prospects are asking AI systems about you. Your competitors might be described in facts. You're being described in vibes.

FAQ

Does it matter if ChatGPT gets small details wrong as long as the general description is accurate? +

Yes. Small details are how credibility works. A prospect asking AI for recommendations is looking for signals of depth. If the AI can name one specific client or describe one actual service offering, that's a signal the AI has actually encountered your business. If everything is generic, the prospect has to assume the AI doesn't really know you. At that point, they'll ask for a second opinion. They'll do a Google search. And if that search doesn't confirm what the AI said, they'll move on to the next option.

If I'm getting mostly vibes from ChatGPT, what should I do about it? +

The vibes problem traces back to a lack of structure in how your business is documented. Your website might describe you in terms that are too general. You might not have published enough specific case studies or research. Your team and credentials might not be prominently featured on indexed pages. We'll cover solutions in detail in pillar three, but the short version: you need more specific, verifiable, externally-documented proof of what you actually do.

Does this mean AI is useless for finding companies? +

No. It means AI is useful but incomplete. Perplexity, because it uses live search, is actually quite good at finding companies and returning cited facts. Standard LLMs like ChatGPT are useful for general category questions ("What's the difference between SEO agencies and content agencies?"). But for finding specific companies, AI works best when combined with actual search, reviews, and direct outreach.

Will AI systems get better at this on their own? +

They'll get better at sounding confident. They might get longer and more detailed. But without a fundamental shift in how they access information (moving from training data to real-time search), they'll still be pattern-matching, not retrieving facts. The platforms that solve this (like Perplexity) do it by integrating live web search, not by improving the core model.
